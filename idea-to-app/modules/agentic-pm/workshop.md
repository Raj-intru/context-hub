# Workshop run sheet — 90 minutes

For a real product team, using their own backlog. **Do not run this with a worked
example** unless you have no choice: teams agree enthusiastically with examples
and change nothing. The argument about their own numbers is the lesson.

**You need:** a product lead with authority to pick, three real candidate items,
and whatever cost data they have (imperfect is fine — discovering it is imperfect
is part of it).

---

## 0 · Before the room (your prep, 20 min)

- Ask the lead for **three candidates for the same outcome**, not three features.
  If they send three unrelated features, that is your first teaching moment —
  keep them and use them to show the difference.
- Ask for one number: what a developer-week costs them, loaded.
- Print or project `scoring.md`'s option table. It is the only artifact that
  matters today.

## 1 · The cycle (10 min)

Show the diagram. Say two things and move on:

> "Everything left of the gate is done by agents. The gate is done by a person
> whose name is on it."

> "Four kinds of work compete in one list — a strategic bet, a customer request,
> tech debt and an analytics finding. Most organisations keep tech debt in a
> separate list, which is a polite way of saying it never has to win."

Do not walk through all six agents. They will nod and learn nothing.

## 2 · Score three real options (35 min) — this is the workshop

Fill the option sheet together, on screen, one column at a time across all three
options. Across, not down: it keeps them comparing.

Expect these four moments, and let each one land:

| What happens | What to say |
| --- | --- |
| They cannot state the **run** cost | "So we know what it costs to build and not what it costs to own. Which of these two numbers does the CFO ask about in year two?" |
| Weeks to users and **weeks to signal** come apart | "Ship in three, know in eleven. Which one were you about to put in the plan?" |
| A number has no source | Strike it out. Visibly. "We'll decide without it rather than pretend." |
| Somebody asks for a total | Go to the rule in `scoring.md`. This is the centre of the session — give it five minutes, not thirty seconds |

If you run long here, run long here. The remaining sections can be cut; this one
cannot.

## 3 · Run Gate A, out loud (20 min)

The lead picks. Then, in front of everyone, says **why not the other two.**

Most leads find this harder than choosing, and the room notices. That discomfort
is the entire argument for the gate — it is the moment a preference has to become
a decision.

Then capture, in writing, before anyone moves on:

- [ ] The option picked, and the date
- [ ] Why not the other two
- [ ] **The metric** — one, named, already instrumented
- [ ] **The Gate B date** — from weeks-to-signal, not weeks-to-users

## 4 · Book Gate B (10 min)

Put the review in calendars **in the room**. Name who presents it. Agree the three
verdicts now, while nothing is at stake — *it worked*, *it did not*, *we cannot
tell* — and that the third is a process failure with a named cause, not a soft
pass.

Agreeing that in advance is the whole trick. Nobody will agree to it in the
meeting where it applies to them.

## 5 · Failure modes (15 min)

Put the eight on screen. Ask: **"Which two do you already have?"**

Then stop talking. The room will diagnose itself more honestly than you can, and
they will argue about it afterwards, which is what you actually came for.

---

## What they leave with

- One option sheet, filled in with their own numbers
- One Gate A decision, signed, with a metric and a date
- A Gate B review in the calendar
- Two named failure modes they admitted to in front of each other

## What to send the next morning

The filled option sheet and the Gate A record. Nothing else — no slides, no
summary of the framework. They have the artifacts of a real decision, and that is
what makes the second cycle happen without you.

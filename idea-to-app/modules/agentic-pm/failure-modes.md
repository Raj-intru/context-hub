# The eight ways this goes wrong

Teach these last, and ask the room which two they already have. Everybody has
two. The useful part of this module is not the diagram — teams agree with the
diagram immediately. It is knowing what it looks like when you are doing it
badly, because every one of these feels like progress from the inside.

---

### 1 · The backlog becomes an inbox

**Symptom:** the ranked list is hundreds long and nobody reads past the top ten.

**Cause:** agents are allowed to create backlog items instead of signals, and
nothing decays.

**Fix:** signals and backlog items are different objects. Signals expire. An item
enters the backlog only when it has been through step 2 with a source attached.

---

### 2 · Scoring theatre

**Symptom:** every option has precise numbers and nobody can say where any of
them came from.

**Cause:** an agent asked for a score will always produce one, and a confident
number looks identical to a researched one.

**Fix:** no score without its working — the metric, the range, the confidence
word, the source and date. An unsourced score is struck out, not discounted. Make
striking one out a normal, unremarkable act in front of the team once, and the
problem stops.

---

### 3 · Gate A becomes a rubber stamp

**Symptom:** "why not the other two" reads *"they scored lower"*.

**Cause:** a composite score, or three options that were never real alternatives.

**Fix:** three columns, never summed. Options must address the same outcome, and
one of them is always "do nothing". If the lead cannot argue the losing case, the
options were decoration.

---

### 4 · Gate B never happens

**Symptom:** you can name what shipped last quarter but not what it moved.

**Cause:** the review date was set after shipping, which means never.

**Fix:** the Gate B date and metric are recorded **at Gate A**, before any work
starts. It is the cheapest discipline in the cycle and the most skipped.

---

### 5 · "We cannot tell" is filed as a win

**Symptom:** outcome reviews conclude "encouraging early signs".

**Cause:** the slice was too big, too slow, or the metric was wrong — and naming
that feels like blame.

**Fix:** treat it as a **process failure with a named cause**, and fix the cause
before the next cycle. This one costs you the cycle *and* the learning, which
makes it the most expensive habit on this page.

---

### 6 · The loop never closes

**Symptom:** the metrics agent exists and its output changes nothing.

**Cause:** no step in the next cycle is required to read it.

**Fix:** the first item on every ranking agenda is last cycle's outcome. If the
previous bet failed, the signal that produced it goes back into step 1 carrying
the evidence — which is a better signal than it was the first time.

---

### 7 · Tech debt quietly leaves the list

**Symptom:** it is "tracked separately for visibility".

**Cause:** it loses on value scoring every time, so somebody mercifully rescues
it from the competition.

**Fix:** keep it in the one list and fix the scoring instead — tech debt's value
is a cost *avoided* and a time-to-market *protected* on everything shipped after
it. Scored that way it wins sometimes, which is the correct answer. If it never
wins, your value model is incomplete, not your priorities.

---

### 8 · The human gate gets automated "temporarily"

**Symptom:** a rule that auto-promotes anything scoring above a threshold, added
during a busy quarter.

**Cause:** the gate is the slowest part of a fast cycle, and the agents are right
often enough to make it feel safe.

**Fix:** name it when it is proposed. The two gates are not overhead in the
framework — **they are the framework.** Everything to the left of them is
research, and research is not a decision no matter how good it is. A cycle with
no human signature is not faster product management; it is a system with nobody
accountable for what it ships.

---

## The one-line test

Ask a team one question: **"Who signed the last bet, and what did they say
afterwards?"**

If there is a name and a sentence, the cycle is working. If the answer is a
process, a tool or a ranking, it is not — whatever the diagram on the wall says.
